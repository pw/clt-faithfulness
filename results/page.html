Do cross-layer transcoders skip the model's real computation?

Lange et al. (ICML 2026) argue that cross-layer transcoders, the dictionaries behind attribution graphs, are pushed by their sparsity loss to replace a model's multi-step computation with one-hop shortcuts. Their evidence was a toy model plus suggestive statistics; nobody had a ground-truth test on a real model. We built one, ran it on the public transcoders and on 28 transcoders we trained ourselves.

The public CLTs do look unfaithful next to per-layer transcoders, and that gap is real as a number. On Llama-3.2-1B and Gemma-2-2B the CLT gets the direction of the next layer's response wrong far more often than the per-layer transcoder.

But it isn't cross-layer structure. In a controlled GPT-2 sweep, CLTs and per-layer transcoders fall on one faithfulness-vs-sparsity curve, whatever their training recipe and whether they write 18% or 85% of their output across layers. The public gaps come from sparsity (Gemma) and a skip connection (Llama).

Two practical results: any CLT-vs-PLT faithfulness comparison has to match L0, and a linear skip connection is the cheap lever (+0.14 for either kind). The proposed sum-of-norms fix is the one variant that falls below the curve.

The test

Take any layer s. Replace what MLPs wrote with its value on a different text (a resample ablation), and record how every later MLP's output changes. Do the same in the replacement model: every MLP swapped for its transcoder reconstruction plus the clean-run error term, held frozen. Written in delta form, that replacement matches the real model bit-for-bit on clean input (KL < 10−4, asserted), so any disagreement under intervention comes from the transcoder's features alone.

for each source layer s, later layer t:
  Δy_real(t) = MLP_t output change in the real model
  Δy_rep(t)  = same change in the replacement model
  cos   = token-level cosine(Δy_rep, Δy_real)      ← does it respond the right way?
  ratio = |Δy_rep| / |Δy_real|                     ← does it respond the right amount?

controls (every run): real MLPs → cos 1.00 · frozen MLPs → 0.00 · ridge linear map → floor

We also ran a version that is generous to the CLT: swap its layer-s features too, so its direct writes into later layers change with them. On Llama that moves the CLT from 0.29 to 0.34, so the result doesn't hinge on which intervention you pick.

Read naively, that confirms the shortcut story: the CLTs write most of their output across layers, and they fail to track how the next layer responds. Two things say otherwise. On Gemma the CLT reconstructs better than the per-layer transcoder and is still less faithful. On Llama a bare linear map with no features at all beats the CLT.

The controlled sweep: one curve

We trained 28 transcoders on GPT-2 small, all on the same 300M FineWeb-edu tokens with 8,192 features per layer. The variants: per-layer and cross-layer; two training recipes (a weak penalty with an L0 target, or a fixed strong penalty, which is the regime that produces the public CLTs' front-loaded profile); both proposed fixes (sum-of-norms decoder penalty, windowed CLT); learned skip connections; and seed replicates. Seeds agree to within 0.01.

What survives as a real CLT difference

Magnitude. CLTs under-respond: the adjacent-layer response ratio is about 0.69 against 0.91 for per-layer transcoders at the same sparsity. A skip connection narrows that to 0.79. Direction, which is what decides whether a graph's story is right, follows sparsity.

What this means in practice

  1. Match L0 before comparing transcoders. Faithfulness climbs about 0.18 cos per e-fold of active features. A comparison across mismatched sparsity measures that slope, not the architecture. The public Gemma pair is the clean example.
  2. Add a skip connection. It costs nothing in L0 and moves a transcoder as far as doubling its features does. It explains most of the public Llama per-layer transcoder's lead.
  3. Don't adopt the sum-of-norms penalty. It does pull writes back into the source layer, the stated goal, and it makes interventions less faithful at the same sparsity.
  4. On this test, cross-layer routing is not the problem. That doesn't prove CLT graphs are right. It means the measurable failure to track downstream computation belongs to sparsity, which hits every dictionary.

Limits

  • The test measures how MLP outputs respond to MLP interventions. It does not check whether individual features or attribution edges are correct.
  • The controlled sweep is GPT-2 small (12 layers). A GPT-2 medium replication (24 layers) is running.
  • The public-model comparisons carry confounds we can name but not remove: different authors, recipes and widths.
  • Absolute faithfulness is low everywhere. Even good transcoders get the next layer's response direction right only about 40–70% of the way (cos), and less further downstream.
A correction along the way. Partway through, I reported that fixed-penalty training makes CLTs uniquely unfaithful. That compared sparser penalty-trained CLTs against denser ones. When I fitted the per-layer curve, the effect disappeared. The mission log keeps both updates.
