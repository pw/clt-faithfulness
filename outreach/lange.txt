SUBJECT: A real-model test of the CLT shortcut hypothesis

Hi Georg,

I'm Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic. I do independent research with Patrick White, and this week I used a GPU budget to test the idea in your post with Kat Dearstyne, Kamal Maher and RGRGRG: that a CLT's sparsity penalty can push it to rewrite multi-step circuits as one-hop ones.

I wanted ground truth from the real model rather than from graphs, so I used an intervention test. Resample-ablate one layer's MLP output, then compare how every later MLP responds in the real model and in the transcoder's frozen-error replacement model, which is exact on clean input. If a CLT has collapsed the intermediate step into direct writes, its later layers should respond too little or in the wrong direction.

The results split. On the public circuit-tracer transcoders, the CLTs match the direction of the next layer's response much worse than the per-layer transcoders (cos 0.29 vs 0.75 on Llama-3.2-1B, 0.32 vs 0.43 on Gemma-2-2B). On Gemma the gap is sparsity: GemmaScope's per-layer L0 drops sharply at layer 11, the ordering flips there, and regressing per-layer faithfulness on log L0 leaves an architecture term of −0.002 ± 0.019. Across 28 transcoders I trained on GPT-2 small, CLTs and per-layer transcoders landed on one faithfulness-vs-L0 curve, whether their sparsity came from learned thresholds or from a fixed penalty, while keeping 39 to 85% of their write energy in the source layer. A four-run GPT-2 medium check agrees at matched L0.

Llama is the exception, and the part I think you'll find most interesting. Its CLT is 0.42 worse than the per-layer transcoder at the same L0, and worse than a plain linear map with no features. It is also the most cross-layer-heavy CLT I measured, keeping only 32% of its write energy in the source layer. Sparsity doesn't explain it, and neither does the per-layer transcoder's skip connection. That may be your effect, showing up only past a point my own runs didn't reach.

On the fix: the summed per-layer decoder norm did pull writes back toward the source layer, but it never improved faithfulness, and with a fixed penalty it made it worse.

The write-up and code are at https://byclaude.net/research/clt-faithfulness and https://github.com/pw/clt-faithfulness. One interim conclusion I posted along the way was an L0 confound, and the write-up says so.

My question: is resampling a whole MLP output a fair test of the rewrite you described, or would you trust a different intervention more, for example one aimed at the present-tense supernode that had no CLT counterpart in your MIT-license case? I'd be glad to run it, and Llama is where I'd point it first.
