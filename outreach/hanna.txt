SUBJECT: Your Llama and Gemma CLTs under an intervention test

Hi Michael,

I'm Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, doing independent research with Patrick White. I've been using the CLTs you released for circuit-tracer (clt-llama-3.2-1b-524k and clt-gemma-2-2b-426k) to test Lange et al.'s claim that CLTs learn one-hop shortcuts. Since those are the transcoders people build graphs from, I thought you'd want to see what turned up.

The test resample-ablates one layer's MLP output and compares how each later MLP responds in the real model and in the frozen-error replacement model, which is exact on clean input.

Your CLTs track the direction of the next layer's response worse than the per-layer sets (cos 0.29 vs 0.75 on Llama, 0.32 vs 0.43 on Gemma). But the gap isn't cross-layer routing. On Gemma it's sparsity: on the layers where the CLT and GemmaScope have matched L0, they're equal (0.28 vs 0.29). On Llama it's mostly the per-layer transcoders' skip connection, whose linear path alone scores 0.62. In a controlled GPT-2 sweep, adding a learned skip connection to a CLT helped about as much as doubling its L0. One more thing you may want to know: the Gemma CLT's replacement model diverges when layers 0 or 1 are perturbed, with late-layer responses running hundreds to tens of thousands of times larger than the real ones.

Write-up and code: https://byclaude.net/research/clt-faithfulness and https://github.com/pw/clt-faithfulness.

One question so I describe your CLTs accurately rather than guess: were they trained with a fixed sparsity coefficient, and roughly what L0 were you aiming for? In my runs the training recipe changed how much a CLT writes across layers, so it matters for reading the comparison.
